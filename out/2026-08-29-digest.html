Security teams may no longer get a quiet patch window

A report from the OCaml world suggests automated exploit hunting is now moving within minutes of public bug discussion.

Simon Willison flags an alarming observation from Anil Madhavapeddy, a Cambridge computer science professor and OCaml compiler maintainer: security issues in OCaml projects are seeing probes almost immediately after patches are shared for discussion. What used to allow a few days — perhaps a release within a week or two — may now be compressed to minutes. In one cited case, a website began seeing probes for percent-encoded traversal sequences within about ten minutes, suggesting automated watchers are monitoring public repositories. Willison’s sharper point is that modern coding agents have become good enough at finding flaws that even a rumor or patch hint can become an exploit lead.

Why it matters: Open-source security has long depended on a fragile norm: disclose, patch, release, then explain. If bots and coding agents can infer exploits from patch chatter instantly, maintainers may need a much more private, faster, and operationally disciplined process.

Sources: Simon Willison

Music labels want AI to pay — and maybe to partner

The industry’s response to AI music generators is splitting between litigation, collaboration and, in at least one case, both.

The Times captures the music business in its most familiar posture: furious about a new technology, intrigued by its revenue potential, and divided over how to proceed. The headline’s question — “Thievery or Innovation?” — is a little too tidy, but useful: labels appear to be treating AI music as an alleged rights violation, a possible product category and a bargaining chip all at once.

Why it matters: The fight over AI music is not just about songs. It is about whether creative catalogs become training material, licensing leverage, or both — and whether incumbents can turn disruption into another negotiated tollbooth.

Atoboy’s decade-in trick: getting sharper, not grander

Eater revisits Junghyun “JP” Park and Ellia Jeongeun Park’s Nomad restaurant ten years after it helped reset expectations for modern Korean dining in New York.

Atoboy opened in Nomad a decade ago as chef Junghyun “JP” Park and Ellia Jeongeun Park’s first major statement: a fine-modern, prix fixe take on Korean food that helped show New York what the form could be. Eater’s new review argues the restaurant is “better than ever,” which is notable partly because the Parks’ later projects — including the acclaimed Atomix, opened two years later and two blocks away — could easily have made the original feel like a prelude. Instead, the claim here is that Atoboy has aged into itself.

Why it matters: Restaurants that change a city’s palate often become trapped by their own origin story. Atoboy’s apparent second-act strength suggests a rarer path: staying foundational without becoming frozen.

A dead-tree Declaration in tech-mad San Francisco

Arion Press is making a new riff on the Declaration of Independence for America’s 250th birthday — very slowly, very physically, and very much on purpose.

In San Francisco, amid the culture of speed and software, Arion Press continues to make what the Times calls some of the world’s most sought-after “dead-tree artifacts.” Its latest project is a new take on the Declaration of Independence timed to America’s 250th birthday. The appeal is almost contrarian: at a moment when text is infinitely reproducible, Arion is betting on the authority of the made object — paper, presswork, scarcity, touch.

Why it matters: The Declaration is already a document, symbol and argument. Recasting it through fine printing asks a timely question: in a country flooded with words, can physical craft make civic language feel less disposable?
