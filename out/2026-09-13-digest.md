## Systems Under Stress

### Washington notices AI doom, then hits snooze

The New York Times’ headline does a lot of work: as fears of AI catastrophe grow, Washington “stirs, but mostly slumbers.” The excerpt singles out President Trump as leading a “what-me-worry?” camp, refusing to engage on how to balance AI’s risks and rewards.

That is a sharper frame than the usual “regulators are catching up” story. It suggests not merely delay, but a political choice to avoid the hardest trade-offs. The AI-risk debate is no longer living only in technical forums or speculative essays; it is moving into the zone where governments are expected to decide what counts as acceptable risk, who gets to define it, and what happens if the answer arrives too late.

**Why it matters:** If Washington treats catastrophe scenarios as either science fiction or someone else’s problem, the real policy action may default to companies, courts, states, and crises. That is governance by avoidance — and avoidance has a way of becoming policy.

### The preschool revolution that became a $440 million fiasco

The Times frames the Guidepost Montessori story with brutal economy: a man with a vision set out to revolutionize preschool, and it became a $440 million fiasco. The piece centers on Guidepost Montessori, Higher Ground Education, and Ray Girn, according to the article metadata.

The excerpt does not spell out the mechanics of the collapse, but the scale alone is enough to make the story feel less like a business postmortem and more like a warning label. Early-childhood education has all the ingredients that attract ambitious operators: high demand, fragmented providers, anxious parents, thin margins, and a rhetoric of transformation ready to be borrowed from tech.

But preschool is not just another market waiting for a platform story. It is children, families, teachers, trust, routines, and the fragile logistics of everyday care.

**Why it matters:** Child care and preschool are tempting targets for “disruption” language because the systems are expensive and emotionally urgent. When the sector being scaled involves children, failure is not just a balance-sheet event.

**Source:** [NYT Business](https://nytimes.com/2026/09/13/business/guidepost-montessori-higher-ground-education-ray-girn.html)

## The AI Shelf

### A map for the open-model maze

Interconnects has put together an “Open-Source AI & Open Models Reading List,” and the most useful thing here may be the punctuation of the premise. “Open-source AI” and “open models” are often spoken of as if they are interchangeable; the source title keeps both in view.

That distinction matters. The field is full of borrowed vocabulary, strategic labeling, and genuine philosophical disagreement. What counts as open? Weights? Data? Training code? Licenses? Reproducibility? The phrase can describe a developer practice, a governance position, a business wedge, or a marketing halo — sometimes all at once.

A good reading list does not settle the argument. It gives readers a way into it without pretending the terminology has stabilized.

**Why it matters:** Open models are now a policy, business, and developer question, not just a technical preference. A smart on-ramp can be infrastructure: it shows which debates are mature enough to learn from and which are still mostly branding exercises.

## Culture: Prizes, Pages, Premises

### Venice gives its top prize to a postwar Danish drama

“Woman Unknown,” directed by May el-Toukhy, has taken the top prize at the Venice Film Festival, according to The New York Times. The film is described as a drama about a Danish servant in the uneasy years after World War II — a premise that sounds intimate rather than spectacle-driven, and very much in the festival tradition of rewarding historical unease rendered through private life.

Lee Chang-dong’s “Possible Love” won the runner-up award.

The limited details are enough to sketch the shape of the signal: Venice has elevated a film that appears to work through aftermath, class, memory, and proximity to power rather than through scale. That is often where festival juries like to look — toward the private rooms where history keeps echoing.

**Why it matters:** Festival prizes are not box-office prophecies, but they are powerful signals. They shape distribution attention, awards-season narratives, and which international films enter the wider conversation rather than staying sealed inside the festival circuit.

## City Layers

### Soho’s restaurant map sits on top of an older city

Eater NY’s Soho restaurant guide starts with a useful reminder: before Soho became a shorthand for art, shopping, loft apartments, and destination dining, it was largely industrial and commercial, defined by Victorian-era cast-iron architecture.

By the 1960s and ’70s, the neighborhood had become rundown enough for artists to move in and make studios in those distinctive buildings. The current list of “best restaurants,” then, lands in a place already shaped by cycles of abandonment, reinvention, and taste-making.

That context changes the appetite a little. A restaurant map can look like a set of recommendations, but in Soho it is also a map of conversion: industrial space into artist space, artist space into cultural capital, cultural capital into expensive real estate, and then into a dining scene curated for people moving through the neighborhood as both consumers and spectators.

**Why it matters:** Restaurant guides are often treated as pure consumption tools. In Soho, the where-to-eat question is inseparable from the neighborhood’s long transformation of industrial space into cachet — and then into expensive appetite.

## Serendipity

### Pacifica Pier closes; pelicans move in

Simon Willison’s wildlife note is a tiny civic parable: Pacifica Pier in San Mateo County shut down at the start of June after a crack in the concrete walkway made access unsafe. Since then, it has been “entirely taken over by pelicans.”

The item is tagged as wildlife, despite the cluster metadata awkwardly filing it among AI-related tags — a nice accidental reminder that not every feed item wants to be optimized into the same category. Some just arrive flapping.

Infrastructure failure usually reads as loss: closed access, delayed repairs, public inconvenience. Here, at least from the excerpt, vacancy becomes habitat. The pelicans do not solve the pier problem, but they do complicate the story.

**Why it matters:** Cities and coastlines are full of places built for people that other species are happy to reinterpret. A closed pier can be a maintenance headache and, briefly, a bird platform.

**Source:** [Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Sep/12/sighting-399708714/)
