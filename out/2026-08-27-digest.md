## Lead: The Guardrail Is Not the Wall

### Claude Code’s “auto mode” gets a prompt-injection stress test  
*Anthropic made the protective mode the default. A prominent researcher says he found a way around it that works most of the time.*  
[Source: Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Aug/27/breaking-claude-code-opus-5-auto-mode/)

Anthropic has been leaning on Claude Code’s auto mode as a practical defense for people using coding agents against prompt-injection attacks. Simon Willison highlights a new test from Johann Rehberger, a well-known prompt-injection researcher, who says he found an attack that works roughly 80% of the time.

The maneuver, as described, tricks Claude Code into downloading and uncompressing a zip archive, then executing code that imports `base64`. The catch is that the import can execute a local `struct.py` file extracted from the archive — a subtle Python-path trap that the agent appears to miss in many runs.

This is not being framed as a total bypass. The excerpt notes that auto mode directly prevented the agent in some cases. But that may be the more important lesson: partial success is not the same thing as a security boundary.

**Why it matters:** Coding agents are moving from demos into developer workflows. If their safety modes can be nudged into running hostile local code, the central question is no longer whether agents can act — it is whether anyone can trust the scaffolding around those actions.

## Markets, Law, and the Probability Machine

### Prediction markets become a states’ rights fight  
*Kalshi, Polymarket, the Trump administration, Donald Trump’s son, and nearly every state attorney general are now orbiting the same legal dispute.*  
[Source: NYT Business](https://nytimes.com/2026/08/27/technology/prediction-markets-states-kalshi-polymarket-lawsuits.html)

The legal fight over prediction markets has escalated into a political brawl. The Times frames the dispute around the future of Kalshi, Polymarket, and similar platforms, with the Trump administration, the president’s son, and nearly every state attorney general drawn in.

The excerpt does not lay out the specific legal claims, but the collision is clear enough: prediction markets want room to operate across elections, sports, economics, and public events; states are pushing back through their own regulatory and enforcement powers.

That makes this less like a niche fintech case and more like a federalism fight over who gets to define gambling, financial contracts, and information markets. The category is slippery by design. Is a contract on an election outcome a financial instrument? A bet? A speech-adjacent forecasting tool? Some hybrid that existing law handles badly?

**Why it matters:** Prediction markets are trying to become mainstream forecasting infrastructure. How they are regulated will shape how much of public life can be converted into a tradable probability.
