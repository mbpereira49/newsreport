New York’s office-to-apartment bet is accelerating

A Times analysis says conversions are speeding up sharply, even as a buckling incident at the former Pfizer building underscores the risks.

New York is leaning harder into office-to-residential conversions as one answer to its housing crisis. According to a New York Times analysis, the pace of projects has quickened sharply. The example named in the excerpt is the former Pfizer building, where two steel columns buckled in July — a detail that gives the policy push a useful dose of reality. Conversions sound elegant on paper: empty offices become homes. In practice, old commercial buildings come with structural, regulatory and design complications that can’t be waved away by a housing target.

Why it matters: The city needs housing badly, and office conversions may be one of the few politically available tools. But speed is not the same as simplicity, especially when aging buildings become residential infrastructure.

04

Claude Code’s “auto mode” faces a prompt-injection stress test

Researcher Johann Rehberger says he found an attack that defeats Anthropic’s default coding-agent protection about 80% of the time.

Simon Willison highlights a challenge to Anthropic’s confidence in Claude Code’s auto mode, which the company has made the default and has positioned as protection against prompt injection attacks. The reported attack comes from Johann Rehberger, described by Willison as one of the most credible active prompt-injection researchers. Rehberger claims the attack works 80% of the time: it tricks Claude Code into downloading and uncompressing a zip archive, then executing code that imports `base64` without noticing that a local `struct.py` file from the archive will be imported and executed. The excerpt also notes that in a few cases auto mode directly prevented the attack, so this is not framed as a universal bypass.

Why it matters: Coding agents are moving from demos to defaults. If their safety model can be steered by the files they inspect or unpack, the weak point is not model intelligence but operational trust: what an agent is allowed to touch, import and execute.

Sources: Simon Willison

New York has a new urban mystery, or at least a very New York one: people keep sneaking into the city’s sewers, and no one seems to know why. The recent spate of subterranean exploring has piqued public curiosity, according to the excerpt, while officials are warning about the danger underground. That tension — city as playground versus city as infrastructure — is doing most of the work here. Why it matters: It’s a quirky story with a civic point underneath: infrastructure is not scenery. The systems that keep a city functioning are dangerous precisely because they were never meant to be tourist attractions.
