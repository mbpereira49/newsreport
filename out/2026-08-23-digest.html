Arizona’s old Colorado River bargain is coming due

A deal that helped fund a massive canal project also put the state first in line for deep-drought water cuts. The Trump administration says it is time.

Arizona’s desert metropolis faces the sharp edge of a decades-old compromise. According to the Times, the state secured funding for a massive Colorado River project by accepting a painful condition: in a severe drought, it would be among the first to lose water. The Trump administration now says that moment has arrived. The excerpt frames this less as a surprise policy shift than as the enforcement of an old bargain made in a wetter political imagination.

Why it matters: Water scarcity is often discussed as a future crisis. This is the more uncomfortable version: past development deals, legal priorities, and infrastructure promises are deciding who absorbs today’s shortages.

Big Tech’s school takeover looks less like disruption than supply-chain control

Google, Microsoft, and peers have used money, reach, and institutional leverage to shape nearly every step of education technology.

The Times’ framing is blunt: companies like Google and Microsoft have “captured” American schools. The mechanism, as described in the excerpt, is not just popular software or cheap devices, but influence across “nearly every step of the education supply chain.” That phrase matters. It suggests a system in which Big Tech is not merely selling tools to schools; it is shaping procurement, classroom practice, administration, and the defaults that students and teachers inherit.

Why it matters: Education technology debates often focus on screen time or privacy. The deeper question is dependency: once a school system is built around a handful of platforms, opting out becomes less a policy choice than a logistical impossibility.

The new coding-agent skill is verification, not line-by-line vigilance

Simon Willison argues that productive AI-assisted programming depends on knowing how to direct agents—and how to prove their work is right.

Simon Willison pushes against a narrow idea of “code review” for AI coding agents. The essential skill, he argues, is confidently instructing agents to make changes and then confidently verifying that those changes landed correctly. Sometimes that still means reading every line. But Willison’s sharper point is that eyeballing code has never been the only—or even the best—way to validate software changes. Tests, behavior, constraints, and review strategies matter more as the volume of agent-written code grows.

Why it matters: If coding agents become normal coworkers, the bottleneck shifts. The valuable engineer is not the person who personally inspects every generated line, but the one who can specify, constrain, and verify systems without being lulled by plausible-looking diffs.

Sources: Simon Willison

The anti-ping backlash has reached carrier-pigeon speed

New apps let people send digital messages very, very slowly—less productivity hack than small rebellion against instant everything.

Some users are embracing messaging apps that deliberately slow digital communication to a crawl. The Times says these ultraslow texting tools have taken off among people “reveling in a slower pace of life,” with the obvious caveat that they are a terrible way to make dinner plans. The appeal is almost comic in its simplicity: keep the convenience of digital writing, remove the tyranny of immediate response.

Why it matters: The interesting trend is not that people want worse messaging. It is that constant reachability has become irritating enough that delay now feels like a feature—and perhaps even a luxury.
