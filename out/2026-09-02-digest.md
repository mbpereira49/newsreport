## Front Page

### Trump leans on regime-change language as the Iran war drags on

Six months into the U.S. conflict with Iran, President Trump is back to one of his most familiar pressure tactics: public maximalism. On Tuesday, he urged Iranians to “rise up and fight,” language that reads less like a new doctrine than a return to a political instinct—say the biggest version of the thing when military and diplomatic pathways appear stuck.

That kind of rhetoric can serve a purpose. It signals resolve. It tries to rattle an adversary. It can give moral clarity to a conflict that otherwise risks becoming a grim procedural grind. But calls for popular revolt also create their own trap: they raise expectations among dissidents, allies and hawks at home about what the United States might do next—and whether it is prepared to back the words with anything more concrete.

The danger is not simply escalation. It is ambiguity dressed as certainty. “Rise up” sounds decisive; policy rarely is.

### Employer health costs may be headed for a 2027 shock

A new survey projects that employer-sponsored health coverage could rise by an average of 11 percent in 2027 unless companies trim benefits. If that number holds, it would be one of the steepest increases in decades—and a reminder that the “employer health plan” is not some neutral workplace perk. For many Americans, it is the health system.

The choices ahead are familiar, and none is painless. Employers can absorb more of the increase, pass costs along through higher worker contributions, raise deductibles, narrow networks, or reduce benefits. In practice, many will likely do some combination of those things.

The broader story is about how health inflation sneaks into everyday life. It may not arrive as a single bill marked “system failure.” It shows up in smaller paychecks, delayed appointments, thinner coverage, and that annual open-enrollment dread when people learn whether their insurance still feels like insurance.

## City Systems Under Stress

### Two deaths in LAPD custody renew scrutiny of mental-health policing

Los Angeles is again asking whether police are being sent into mental-health crises with the wrong tools, training, or mandate. The deaths of two men in LAPD custody have triggered investigations after the city released graphic video of officers’ actions. Mayor Karen Bass questioned whether officers are adequately prepared to handle these calls.

That question is both immediate and structural. The immediate version is about the two men who died, the officers involved, and what the investigations find. The structural version is older and harder: why armed police remain the default responders to so many episodes of distress, disorientation and crisis.

Custody deaths strike at the most basic promise the state makes when it takes control of a person: that they will survive the encounter. When mental health is part of the call, the issue expands beyond discipline and training. It becomes a design problem for the whole emergency-response system.

### New York’s ultra-rich keep pulling away

New York City’s richest residents have grown even richer, according to a new report from the city comptroller. The report drills down to the top .001 percent—a sliver so small it almost stops feeling like a demographic category and starts sounding like a rounding error. But the money is very real, and the conclusion is blunt: income inequality remains deeply entrenched.

New York has always turned proximity into drama: luxury towers above struggling storefronts, private wealth beside public systems under pressure. What the report suggests is that the top is not merely staying high; it is continuing to separate.

That matters because nearly every major city debate runs through this divide. Housing policy, tax politics, public transit, schools, policing, cultural life—each is shaped by the same underlying question: who can still afford to belong to the city, and on what terms?

## Technology & Copyright Weather

### Claude’s system prompt draws a bright line around song lyrics

Simon Willison notes that Anthropic continues to publish system prompts for its Claude consumer apps, now organized by model and including historical versions. That transparency is unusual and useful. It turns a normally hidden layer of AI behavior into something the public can inspect, compare and argue about.

The standout detail in the newer Claude prompt: a conspicuous insistence on not reproducing song lyrics. The instruction appears to have become especially emphatic, which is interesting not because song lyrics are the only copyright issue in AI, but because they are a particularly vivid one. Everyone understands the difference between “tell me about a song” and “print the song.”

System prompts are not policy memos exactly, but they are policy in executable form. They show how a company translates legal anxiety, product goals and safety posture into words a model is supposed to obey. In this case, the boundary is being drawn with a thick marker.

## Media Shelf

### *The Saturday Evening Post* is ending print after 205 years

After 205 years, *The Saturday Evening Post* will stop printing. It will continue online, which is both a continuation and a rupture: the title survives, but the object that carried its identity for generations does not.

The Post’s history gives the news its extra weight. This was a magazine associated with Jack London stories, Norman Rockwell covers and a certain mass-market image of American life. Its print edition was not merely a delivery mechanism; it was part of the meaning.

The end of a print run is often explained through ad economics, production costs and reader habits. All true. But some closures carry symbolic force beyond the balance sheet. This one feels like another line being drawn between “publication” and “printed thing”—two ideas that once seemed inseparable.

### Cleo gets the critic’s nod for Lebanese-spiced chicken

Cleo in the West Village has landed a critic’s pick for a menu centered on chicken rubbed with a warming Lebanese spice blend. The pitch is satisfyingly direct: go for the bird, and do not ignore the crinkle-cut fries.

In New York dining, a critic’s pick can change the temperature around a place fast. The most powerful restaurant hooks are often the simplest ones—not an abstract concept, not a labored philosophy, but a dish people can picture before they book. Spiced chicken and fries is legible in exactly that way.

There is no real competing account here; the second source is essentially a syndicated pointer to the same review. The useful takeaway is straightforward: Cleo now has the kind of praise that can turn a neighborhood table into a reservation problem.

## A Little Good News, Earned

### The kākāpō count reaches 325

Here is the rare conservation update that deserves its brightness: the kākāpō population has reached 325. Andrew Digby reported that chicks from this year’s record breeding season have now become juveniles and been added to the count. In 1995, just 51 kākāpō remained.

Simon Willison quoted the update as “the best news of the year,” and the numbers make the optimism feel justified rather than sentimental. Recovery for a critically endangered species is slow, fragile, managerial work—the kind that depends on years of vigilance rather than one heroic intervention.

That is what makes the figure land. It is not a miracle. It is evidence that sustained care can move a species from managed decline toward rebound. Not safe, not finished, but meaningfully more alive in the world.
