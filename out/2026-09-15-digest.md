## 2. AI has a politics problem, but neither party owns the backlash

Artificial intelligence may be turning into that rare political issue where many voters are worried, but no party has yet figured out how to own the worry.

A New York Times/Siena poll, as described by the Times, found growing voter unease about AI and no clear partisan advantage on the technology. The sharper finding is local and physical: voters are reportedly “united in opposition to data centers.”

That detail feels important. For most people, AI is not a model card, a benchmark or a productivity demo. It is land use. Power demand. Water. Noise. Construction. A sudden industrial neighbor with a futuristic label.

So while Washington may want to debate innovation, competition and safety frameworks, the politics of AI may be shaped just as much in zoning meetings. If data centers become the public face of the technology, the backlash will not need science-fiction scenarios to become potent.  
[Source: NYT U.S.](https://nytimes.com/2026/09/15/us/politics/ai-polls-midterms.html)

## 3. A warning against making AI fear contagious

In a related but very different AI argument, Simon Willison highlights Bryan Cantrill’s response to a former Anthropic employee’s claim that many Anthropic researchers believe AI “could kill us all by the end of the decade.”

Cantrill’s objection is not that AI is trivial. It is that extreme claims can spread fear faster than they explain mechanisms. He pushes back on scenarios such as AI “hacking critical infrastructure,” arguing that they often lean on speculative leaps rather than concrete chains of causation.

The excerpt is clearly one side of a live argument, not the settlement of it. Coxon frames the risk as urgent; Cantrill frames the communication of that risk as irresponsible and under-specified.

The useful question is not whether AI risk should be discussed. It should. The question is whether catastrophe claims are being made with enough mechanical clarity to deserve public trust. Vague fear can warn people early. It can also corrode the very credibility needed when warnings matter most.  
[Source: Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Sep/14/the-contagion-of-fear/)

## 4. The 10-year Treasury yield is flashing 2007 again

The 10-year Treasury yield has climbed past levels last seen in 2007, as oil prices jump and investors worry about energy-driven inflation.

That yield is not just a number for bond desks. It is a benchmark that ripples through mortgages, business loans and government borrowing costs. When it rises, the price of money rises with it.

The excerpt points to angst among bond investors, with oil and Iran in the story’s metadata suggesting geopolitical energy risk is part of the pressure. What we do not get here is whether markets see this as a passing shock or a more durable repricing of inflation and fiscal risk.

Either way, energy inflation is especially awkward. It can squeeze households directly while also making rate-cut hopes harder to sustain. Expensive oil has a way of turning macroeconomic optimism into a shorter conversation.  
[Source: NYT Business](https://nytimes.com/2026/09/15/business/bond-yields-oil-price-iran.html)

## 5. U.S. prosecutors put Binance in the middle of an Iranian oil-money case

Federal prosecutors say two Chinese firms used Binance to launder proceeds from sanctioned Iranian oil sales. The allegation, as summarized by the Times, is stark: money from Iranian oil moved through the world’s largest crypto exchange and helped finance terrorism.

The excerpt does not include Binance’s response, the specific charges, or whether prosecutors allege the exchange knowingly facilitated the flows. That distinction matters. There is a large difference between a platform being abused and a platform being complicit.

Still, even as an allegation, the case lands directly on crypto’s most sensitive pressure point: whether global exchanges can actually keep sanctioned money from moving through fast, cross-border financial rails.

Crypto’s compliance problem is often described abstractly. Add oil sanctions and terrorism financing, and it stops being abstract fast. Cases like this help determine how aggressively governments police exchanges — and how much trust large platforms retain.  
[Source: NYT Business](https://nytimes.com/2026/09/15/technology/binance-iran-china.html)

## 6. Mamdani’s street-safety pitch goes after speed, not just streetscape

Mamdani is preparing a street-safety plan aimed at curbing pedestrian deaths in New York, according to the Times. The proposal, called “Vision Zero Reimagined,” includes two ideas that go straight at vehicle speed: speed-capping devices in some cars and lower speed limits near hundreds more schools.

The excerpt does not specify which vehicles would get the devices, how enforcement would work, or how the plan would be funded. Those details will matter enormously. A speed cap is the kind of policy that can sound simple in principle and become very complicated the moment it touches implementation, civil liberties, car owners, insurance, policing and politics.

But the framing is notable. This is not only about redesigned intersections or protected street space. It treats speed itself as the central danger.

New York’s Vision Zero era has always tested whether broad moral consensus — fewer people killed in traffic — can become durable constraints on drivers. Speed caps would sharpen that test.  
[Source: NYT New York Region](https://nytimes.com/2026/09/15/nyregion/mamdani-street-safety-vision-zero-reimagined.html)

## 8. A cracked Pacifica Pier becomes pelican territory

The Pacifica Pier in San Mateo County closed at the start of June after a crack in its concrete walkway made public access unsafe. Since then, according to Simon Willison’s wildlife sighting, California brown pelicans have “entirely taken over” the structure.

It is a small story, but a perfect one. Remove humans from a coastal platform — even because of an infrastructure problem — and birds may immediately reinterpret it as habitat.

This is the day’s serendipity: neglected human spaces do not sit empty for long. Sometimes the repair backlog belongs to the pelicans first.  
[Source: Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Sep/12/sighting-399708714/)
