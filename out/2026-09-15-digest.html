AI has a politics problem, but neither party owns the backlash

A Times/Siena poll finds broad voter unease about artificial intelligence — and unified opposition to data centers.

Artificial intelligence may be the rare issue where alarm is widespread but partisan advantage is still up for grabs. A New York Times/Siena poll, as described by NYT, found growing voter fears about AI and no clear party edge on the technology. The sharper finding: voters are “united in opposition to data centers,” suggesting the most tangible local face of AI — land, power, water, noise, construction — may be more politically potent than abstract arguments about productivity or existential risk.

Why it matters: AI policy is moving from white papers to zoning meetings. If data centers become the symbol of the technology, the politics of AI may be shaped less by innovation rhetoric than by local resistance to infrastructure.

The excerpt does not specify which vehicles would get the devices, how enforcement would work, or how the plan would be funded. Why it matters: New York’s Vision Zero era has often been judged by whether it can turn moral consensus — fewer people killed in traffic — into politically durable constraints on drivers. Speed caps would test that consensus.

The 10-year Treasury yield is flashing 2007 again

A jump in oil prices is feeding inflation worries and pushing a key U.S. borrowing rate toward a 20-year high.

The 10-year Treasury yield has breached levels last seen in 2007, NYT reports, as oil prices jump and investors worry about energy-driven inflation. That yield is a benchmark for borrowing costs across the economy, so the move is more than a bond-market curiosity. The excerpt points to angst among bond investors, with oil and Iran in the story’s metadata suggesting geopolitical energy risk is part of the pressure. What is not provided here: whether markets see this as a temporary shock or a more durable repricing of inflation and fiscal risk.

Why it matters: When the 10-year yield rises, mortgages, business financing and government debt costs all feel it. Energy inflation is especially awkward because it can squeeze households while making rate-cut hopes harder to sustain.

A cracked Pacifica Pier becomes pelican territory

After the pier closed over safety concerns, California brown pelicans moved in — a small, perfect bit of urban wildlife opportunism.

The Pacifica Pier in San Mateo County shut down at the start of June after a crack in its concrete walkway made public access unsafe. Since then, according to Simon Willison’s wildlife sighting, California brown pelicans have “entirely taken over” the structure. It is a tiny story, but a pleasingly concrete one: remove humans from a coastal platform, even for an infrastructure problem, and birds may immediately reinterpret it as habitat.

Why it matters: This is the day’s serendipity: a reminder that neglected or closed human spaces do not sit empty for long. Sometimes the repair backlog belongs to the pelicans first.
