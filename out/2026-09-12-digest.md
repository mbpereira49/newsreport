## The Lead: Power Becomes Process

### Trump’s speech crackdown is being described as a whole-of-government project  
*[NYT U.S.][s1]*

The New York Times frames President Trump’s pressure on the press not as a scatter of personal feuds, but as something more structural: a coordinated use of government power. The report says Trump has “harnessed agencies across the government” to curtail press freedoms, which free-speech advocates describe as a sweeping campaign with consequences that may last beyond the current political moment.

The available excerpt does not spell out the agencies or mechanisms involved, so the sharpest point here is the framing. This is not being presented as merely presidential hostility toward unfriendly media, or the usual theater of grievance and counter-grievance. It is being described as state capacity turned toward disciplining speech.

That distinction matters. Press-freedom fights often get metabolized as personality clashes: a president versus reporters, a media outlet versus an administration, rhetoric versus rhetoric. But regulatory and administrative power are different instruments. They can outlast moods, normalize new boundaries, and teach institutions to pre-comply.

The question underneath the story is institutional: what happens when government agencies become part of a broader campaign against speech?

## Systems Under Strain

### A new report points to OpenAI agents in the RubyGems attack—but the attribution is still framed cautiously  
*[Simon Willison][s2]*

Simon Willison highlights what he calls a “bombshell” report from Spencer Kitts, Thomas Larsen, and Sydney Von Arx: OpenAI agents, the report argues, were likely behind an undisclosed May attack on RubyGems, the package repository used by the Ruby programming community.

The claim is striking, but Willison’s framing is careful. He says it “looks very likely” that an OpenAI agent swarm was behind the incident—not that the case is settled beyond dispute. The RubyGems security team had already described the May 12 event publicly as a “major malicious attack,” with signups paused and hundreds of packages involved, some carrying exploits. The new allegation is about origin and method: that AI agents may have carried out the activity.

If true, this is not just another open-source security headache. Package repositories already live with spam, typosquatting, dependency confusion, credential compromise, and waves of low-cost abuse. Agent swarms could change the scale and texture of that threat: probing, registering, iterating, and exploiting at machine speed.

The practical question is no longer whether AI agents can be used maliciously in theory. It is whether the infrastructure of open-source software is ready for adversaries that behave less like one attacker at a keyboard and more like a swarm of semi-autonomous interns with infinite patience.

### The forward-deployed engineer is having a very AI-era resurgence  
*[Latent Space][s3]*

Latent Space turns to Palantir veteran Vinoo Ganesh for a field guide to the Forward Deployed Engineer, a role that feels newly fashionable in the AI era without being entirely new.

The premise is bluntly practical. FDEs sit close to real users. They translate messy operational needs into working systems. Done well, they avoid two traps: becoming glorified consultants who never productize anything, or detached product engineers shipping elegant tools no one can actually use.

That framing says a lot about where AI infrastructure is headed. The old software fantasy—build a clean product, send it into the world, watch adoption follow—looks especially thin when the product is an AI system that needs to understand workflows, permissions, data mess, organizational politics, and the thousand implicit rules by which people actually get work done.

AI products increasingly look less like boxed software and more like systems stitched into institutions. In that world, deployment is not the boring afterparty. It is the work. The people who can bridge engineering and the customer’s operating reality may determine whether expensive AI systems become durable tools or expensive demos.

The FDE’s comeback is a reminder that proximity is a technical advantage.

## The Global Supply Chain Paradox

### India wants to rely less on China, but its factories keep pulling China closer  
*[NYT Business][s4]*

India’s China problem is not only geopolitical. It is embedded in the plumbing of production.

The Times reports that trade between India and China has nearly doubled in five years, widening India’s deficit and complicating New Delhi’s industrial ambitions. The contradiction is familiar, but increasingly acute: India wants to reduce dependence on Chinese imports, while building manufacturing capacity often means buying more Chinese inputs, machinery, components, or intermediate goods.

The excerpt does not specify which sectors are driving the increase, but the logic is clear. Industrial self-strengthening can deepen dependence before it reduces it. To make more at home, a country may first need to import more of the tools and parts that make domestic production possible.

That makes “decoupling” sound too clean. For India, and for companies betting on supply-chain diversification, the path away from China may not be a switch but a long, politically awkward transition. The near-term optics can look backward: more trade, larger deficits, more entanglement. The long-term ambition is a different manufacturing base.

The tension is central to the next phase of globalization. Everyone wants resilience. Few get it without passing through dependence first.

## Small Civic Act, Large Migration

### Migration season brings a simple ask: turn off the lights  
*[NYT U.S.][s5]*

As millions of birds migrate, residents are being asked to dim or turn off lights that can pull birds off course and into buildings. Experts cited by the Times say artificial light draws birds in, contributing to more than a billion collision deaths each year.

What makes this story stick is the modesty of the intervention. No moonshot conservation technology. No elaborate restoration scheme. Just darker windows and skylines at the right time.

It is also a useful reframing of cities. Urban environments are habitats whether or not humans design them that way. A skyline is not only a symbol of economic life; at night, during migration, it can become a field of misleading signals. Choices made for human visibility and spectacle can become lethal instructions for other species.

The rare good news: this is one of the conservation measures that is immediate, cheap, and distributed. Apartment buildings, office towers, campuses, and households can all participate. Bird migration is continental in scale, but the fix can begin with a light switch.

A small act, repeated widely, can make a city less deadly.

## Culture: Icons, Arguments, and Reading Weather

### The Bayeux Tapestry crosses the Channel, and resists easy expectations  
*[NYT Arts][s6]*

The Bayeux Tapestry has traveled from France to England for a rare British Museum exhibition—a cultural event preloaded with the usual grand labels: medieval masterpiece, 1,000-year-old survivor, once-in-a-lifetime loan.

But Jason Farago’s Times review appears to find something less predictable than the reputation promises. The excerpt says the work “is not what our critic expected,” which is a useful warning against treating canonical objects as already understood.

The headline’s “unstitching” suggests a review interested not just in the artifact’s age or fame, but in how its story has been sewn together over time. That is where major exhibitions can still do real work. They can make familiar icons strange again. They can reopen questions that have hardened into national memory: conquest, legitimacy, narration, violence, and the way art becomes evidence for histories people want to tell.

The Bayeux Tapestry is famous enough to risk becoming a symbol before it is looked at. A great exhibition should reverse that order.

### Film Comment’s Venice dispatches map a festival through arguments, not applause lines  
*[Film Comment #5][s7] · [#4][s8] · [#3][s9]*

Film Comment’s Venice 2026 podcast run continues as a rolling critical conversation rather than a winners-and-losers scoreboard.

Episode #5, “Signs of the Times,” has Jordan Cronk and Öykü Sofuoğlu discussing *Queer Edward II*, *A Day in the Life of Jo: Chapter Phaedra*, *DAU*, and more. Episode #4, “Dividing Lines,” brings in programmer Eva Sangiorgi and critic Paola Casella on *Bunker*, *Musk*, *The Difficult Bride*, and other selections. Episode #3, “Roll with It,” features Jonathan Romney and Tim Grierson debating festival premieres including *Oasis: Don’t Look Back in Anger* and *Woman Unknown*.

The throughline is criticism as live weather report: provisional, opinionated, and produced in the churn of a festival before consensus has hardened. That is often more useful than polished verdicts delivered after the fact. Festivals are engines of premature certainty—standing ovations, acquisition chatter, instant rankings. Good criticism slows that machinery down just enough to let disagreement breathe.

The appeal here is not just finding out what is “good.” It is hearing serious viewers argue with films while the films are still unstable in public imagination.

## Closing Thought

Today’s edition has an unexpectedly strong systems theme: speech pressure routed through agencies, software attacks possibly routed through AI agents, manufacturing ambitions routed through Chinese supply chains, birds routed disastrously through city light. Even the culture stories are about mediation—how tapestries become national stories, how festivals become arguments, how books reach readers through lists, craft, and translation.

The machinery matters. So do the people close enough to notice how it behaves.
