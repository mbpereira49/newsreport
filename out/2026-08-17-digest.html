Flue 2 wants to be React for agents

Fred Schott’s agent harness borrows hooks from frontend development — a telling sign of where AI engineering is maturing.

Latent Space profiles Flue 2, an agent framework from Astro creator Fred Schott that takes direct inspiration from React. The core idea is less about yet another agent demo and more about the “harness”: the runtime, structure, and developer ergonomics that define what an agent can reliably do. Schott has added hooks, borrowing one of React’s most influential abstractions, to organize agent behavior. That analogy is doing a lot of work. Frontend development got dramatically more legible once teams converged around component models, lifecycle patterns, and state management. Agent infrastructure is now hunting for its own equivalents.

Why it matters: The agent story is shifting from flashy autonomy to boring-but-crucial software architecture. If agents become useful, it may be because their harnesses become as disciplined as UI frameworks.

Sources: Latent Space

The New York Times reports that educators are increasingly worried many students can no longer write essays without AI. The concern is not just mechanical — spelling, grammar, structure — but developmental. Writing has long functioned as a way to clarify thought, build argument, and practice intellectual stamina. If students outsource that process too early or too often, the loss may be harder to measure than a bad paragraph but more consequential. The excerpt does not tell us how widespread the problem is, and schools are likely seeing uneven patterns. Still, the anxiety is clear: AI assistance is forcing educators to decide what writing is for.

Why it matters: This is one of the central AI-in-education questions: whether tools that improve output also weaken the cognitive practice that output used to prove.

Meet Morris, the Pacific’s unlikely Northern Gannet

A lone white seabird with a yellow head has become a local celebrity in Pillar Point Harbor.

For today’s small wonder: Simon Willison posts about Morris, a Northern Gannet in Pillar Point Harbor near Half Moon Bay. The bird is apparently the only known Northern Gannet in the entire Pacific Ocean. Morris first showed up around the Farallon Islands off San Francisco 14 years ago and has since made the harbor home. The field mark is charmingly simple: look for the only white bird with a yellow head, usually hanging out with smaller black Brandt’s cormorants near the harbor sign visible from the commercial pier.

Why it matters: Not every notable migration is human or technological. Morris is a reminder that anomalies become landmarks when a community notices them long enough.

Sources: Simon Willison
