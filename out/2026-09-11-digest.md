**Source:** [NYT U.S.][afghan-court]

A little-known terrorism court has now been used to deport an Afghan woman, according to the New York Times. The details available here are thin: the excerpt does not lay out the allegations, the woman’s circumstances, or the procedural record in any depth. What it does make clear is that the administration successfully obtained an expulsion order from the court’s chief judge.

## Money, odds, and who gets to set the rules

### Inflation puts a rate hike back on the table  
*Markets now see a quarter-point Fed increase next week as overwhelmingly likely after August’s CPI report.*  
**Source:** [NYT Business][inflation-cpi]

August’s Consumer Price Index report appears to have moved the Federal Reserve conversation sharply. The New York Times reports that market odds of a quarter-point rate increase at next week’s Fed meeting jumped to 90 percent after the inflation data.

That is not just “inflation came in hot” territory. It means the report was strong enough to reprice expectations around the Fed’s next move. The central bank’s familiar bind is still the central problem: inflation that remains elevated limits its room to protect growth.

**Why it matters:** A rate hike would travel quickly through mortgages, credit cards, business borrowing, and asset prices. Even the expectation of higher rates can tighten financial conditions before the Fed formally acts.

### Minnesota draws the line on prediction markets  
*Its ban on Kalshi and Polymarket has turned into a test case over who gets to regulate event-betting platforms.*  
**Source:** [NYT Business][prediction-markets]

Minnesota has become the first state to pass a law banning Kalshi and Polymarket, putting it at the center of the fight over prediction markets. The New York Times frames the next stage as a jurisdictional battle: a lawsuit filed by a federal agency may set a precedent for whether states can police these markets or whether federal regulators occupy the field.

The excerpt does not specify the agency or the claims, but the stakes are clear enough. Prediction markets keep slipping between categories: finance, gambling, forecasting, entertainment, information aggregation. Each label implies a different regulator, and each regulator implies a different future.

**Why it matters:** This is not just about two platforms in one state. The Minnesota case could help determine whether event-betting markets are governed state by state, under federal authority, or through some still-awkward hybrid.

## The AI Desk: breakthroughs, openness, and trust

### AI’s claimed Navier–Stokes breakthrough arrives under a cloud  
*Simon Willison points to an impressive OpenAI result — and to accusations that complicate the celebration.*  
**Source:** [Simon Willison][navier-stokes]

Simon Willison’s post describes an extraordinary claim: OpenAI used an unreleased model to produce a resolution to the Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness problem, one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems carrying a $1 million prize.

That would be a landmark result if it holds. But, as Willison frames it, the discovery is “somewhat overshadowed” by accusations of skulduggery from NYU mathematician Tristan Buckmaster, who had been collaborating on related problems with Levent Alpöge, a mathematician now at Anthropic. Buckmaster’s complaint came alongside a hastily published version of their own results.

The supplied excerpt cuts off mid-explanation, so the precise substance and merits of the dispute remain unclear. That caveat matters. In a story this large, both the math and the process need independent daylight.

**Why it matters:** If the result is sound, it would be enormous for mathematics and for AI-assisted discovery. If the path to the result is contested, it becomes something else too: a test of credit, secrecy, and trust when frontier labs enter high-stakes research.

## New York, memory, appetite

### The long afterlife of post-9/11 Islamophobia in New York  
*A generation of Muslim and Sikh New Yorkers was shaped by backlash — but the story is not only one of victimization.*  
**Source:** [NYT New York Region][nyc-islamophobia]

The New York Times looks at how the years after 9/11 shaped Muslim and Sikh New Yorkers. The excerpt describes a wave of hate following the attacks, while also noting that Muslim Americans’ political and cultural influence has grown in the decades since.

That dual frame is important. Anniversary stories can flatten history into ritual: trauma remembered, resilience invoked, lesson stated. This one, at least in outline, points toward a more complicated civic arc — communities targeted by suspicion and violence also becoming more visible, organized, and influential in public life.

**Why it matters:** The legacy of 9/11 is not only geopolitical or memorial. It lives in neighborhoods, identities, institutions, and the public standing of communities that were forced to navigate fear while building power.
