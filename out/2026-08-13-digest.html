New York’s coverage cliff arrives

More than 400,000 New Yorkers are hunting for health insurance—or going without—after federal cuts hit a state plan for working-class residents.

New York’s unusually generous health plan for working-class people has been hit hard by federal funding cuts, according to the report. The result is not an abstract budget fight: more than 400,000 people are now trying to find replacement coverage or living uninsured. The political framing is plain in the story’s premise—Trump-era cuts are now showing up as household-level trade-offs, especially for people who were not poor enough for the most limited safety nets but not financially secure enough to absorb the loss of coverage.

Why it matters: Health policy often becomes real only when enrollment letters arrive. This is a large-scale test of how quickly a state can patch a hole created by federal retrenchment—and who falls through while it tries.

Homeland Security spent $464 million through a no-bid contract for 10 used jets it said were urgently needed. The fleet has largely sat idle for months.

The Department of Homeland Security used a no-bid contract to buy 10 used jets for deportation flights, saying the planes were urgently needed. But the report says the $464 million fleet has mostly been parked for months. That gap—between emergency procurement and actual use—is the story: an expensive piece of immigration-enforcement infrastructure acquired under urgency, then apparently underused.

Why it matters: No-bid contracts always ask for trust; idle assets ask whether that trust was earned. The episode is a clean window into how the politics of deportation can translate into costly, fast-moving procurement decisions.

The Supreme Court’s pandemic improvisation stuck

Live audio and a revised argument format have changed the court’s most public ritual—opening it up while unsettling its old choreography.

A Covid-era scramble has reshaped Supreme Court oral arguments. Live audio, a new format and a broader set of voices have transformed what the report calls the court’s signature public ceremony. Treasury backs off shell-company transparency

The Trump administration will not enforce Corporate Transparency Act reporting rules meant to help crack down on money laundering.

The Treasury Department is scaling back scrutiny of U.S. shell companies by declining to enforce reporting requirements under the 2021 Corporate Transparency Act. The law was designed to crack down on money laundering by requiring more disclosure from business entities. The move does not repeal the law, based on the excerpt, but nonenforcement can be functionally powerful: a transparency regime without enforcement is, at minimum, a much softer deterrent.

Why it matters: Anonymous shell companies are a favored tool for hiding money. Pulling back on disclosure enforcement shifts the balance toward privacy for owners—and away from investigators trying to trace illicit finance.

Sources: NYT Business
