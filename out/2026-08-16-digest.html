A parasite outbreak exposes the lag in food-safety tracing

Cyclospora sickened thousands; critics say delayed rules and weakened oversight made finding the source harder.

The Times reports that Cyclospora evaded the U.S. food-safety system and sickened thousands, with critics pointing to eroded oversight and a delayed Trump administration rule that could have helped investigators identify the contamination source faster. The excerpt is careful: it says the rule “could have sped up” the effort, not that it would definitely have prevented illness. That distinction matters. Food-safety failures are often systems failures—slow traceback, fragmented supply chains, limited data—not a single missed switch. Still, the political charge is direct: regulatory delay may have made an already difficult investigation worse.

Why it matters: Food safety is invisible when it works and brutally tangible when it fails. The story suggests that traceability rules are not bureaucratic ornament; they can determine how quickly public-health officials stop an outbreak.

Flue 2 borrows React’s mental model for AI agents

Fred Schott’s agent harness adds hooks, betting that agents are defined less by prompts than by the systems around them.

Latent Space’s interview with Fred Schott, best known for Astro, frames Flue 2 as “React for Agents.” The excerpt says the new version takes inspiration from React, adds hooks, and advances the idea that agents are defined by their harnesses. That last phrase is the useful provocation. In agent infrastructure, the model is only one component; state, tool use, memory, orchestration, retries, and developer ergonomics shape what the agent can reliably do. The React analogy suggests a push toward composable patterns that software developers already understand, rather than bespoke agent spaghetti.

Why it matters: As agent hype matures, the interesting work is moving into harnesses: the frameworks that make model behavior inspectable, reusable, and less chaotic. Flue 2 is one signal of that shift.

Meet Morris, the Pacific’s improbable Northern Gannet

A lone white seabird with a yellow head has become a Pillar Point Harbor celebrity after turning up near San Francisco 14 years ago.

Simon Willison’s sighting note is the day’s small marvel. Morris, a Northern Gannet—Morus bassanus—is described as the only known one in the entire Pacific Ocean. They appeared in the Farallon Islands off San Francisco 14 years ago and have since made Pillar Point Harbor their home. The field mark is wonderfully simple: look for the only white bird with a yellow head, usually hanging out near smaller black Brandt’s cormorants by the harbor sign visible from the end of the commercial pier. In a feed otherwise full of AI, politics, and institutional failure, Morris is a useful interruption: a singular bird, wildly out of range, quietly becoming local infrastructure.

Why it matters: Serendipity has civic value too. A lone seabird can turn a harbor into a place of attention, habit, and shared local lore.
