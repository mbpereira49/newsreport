The basic pattern: resellers offer access to an LLM proxy at steep discounts to official API pricing, with supply allegedly coming from abused free trials, unprotected support bots, stolen credit cards, or chargeback attacks. Why it matters: This is the unglamorous backside of AI platform growth: wherever tokens are valuable, someone will build a market to acquire and launder them. For model providers, that turns fraud prevention, bot hardening, and key management into core revenue protection—not just security hygiene.

The concern, as framed in the excerpt, is not merely that people are gambling on health outcomes; it is that markets tied to research milestones could create incentives that rub uneasily against the norms of clinical science.

Letting traders bet directly on those milestones may surface useful signals—but it also raises obvious worries about leaks, manipulation, and pressure around already fragile research processes.

School districts are becoming housing developers

As housing costs make it harder for teachers to live near their jobs, more districts are trying a blunt fix: build homes themselves.

The New York Times reports that an increasing number of school districts are building housing to recruit and retain teachers. The reason is straightforward: the national housing shortage has made it difficult for essential workers, including educators, to live in the communities where they work. The idea sounds unusual only until you strip it down. If wages cannot keep pace with local rents, and the labor shortage is severe enough, employers with land or borrowing capacity start acting like housing providers.

Why it matters: Teacher housing is a local workaround for a national affordability problem. It may help districts compete for staff, but it also shows how distorted the housing market has become: schools are being pushed beyond education policy into real estate because the surrounding economy no longer supports the workforce they need.

Sources: NYT Business
