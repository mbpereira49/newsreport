## The Escalation Stack

### U.S. strikes Iran, but leaves the target list vague  
*Source: [NYT U.S.][iran-strikes]*

The United States has launched strikes inside Iran aimed at the Islamic Revolutionary Guards Corps, according to U.S. Central Command. That headline is already enough to move the room. But the most consequential detail is the one CENTCOM did **not** provide: what, exactly, the attacks were intended to destroy.

That omission matters. Without a clear description of the military objective, scale or intended effect, the action is defined less by what happened than by the category of target — the IRGC. Was this a narrow operation? A deterrent signal? A prelude? The public record, at least from the excerpt available, does not say.

A U.S. strike inside Iran is inherently escalatory. Specificity is not just a communications nicety here; it is part of how governments, markets and adversaries judge intent. Vague targeting language leaves more room for competing interpretations — and in a crisis, interpretation can become its own accelerant.

### More tankers hit near Hormuz, and oil prices jump  
*Source: [NYT Business][tankers-hormuz]*

Two more oil tankers were attacked near Oman on Monday, adding to a series of strikes on vessels in the Persian Gulf. Oil prices rose more than 2 percent after the news.

The excerpt does not identify who carried out the attacks or describe the damage. But the geography does much of the work: the Strait of Hormuz is one of the world’s critical energy chokepoints, and violence near it tends to travel fast — from ship decks to commodity screens to inflation expectations.

This is how regional conflict becomes global weather. A tanker attack is not just a maritime security incident; it is a potential tax on transportation, manufacturing and household budgets. Even a modest oil move can reshape political pressure if it lingers.

## Pressure Systems

### The bond market is making debt more expensive everywhere  
*Source: [NYT Business][bond-yields]*

A global bond sell-off is pushing government bond yields to multi-decade highs, as investors worry about debt levels, deficits and inflation. That may sound like the language of terminals and treasury desks, but it rarely stays there for long.

Government bond yields are benchmarks. When they rise, the effects can seep into mortgages, business loans and other forms of credit. The market’s judgment on public borrowing becomes a private-sector reality: higher financing costs, tougher investment decisions, more expensive homes.

The important thing here is not just that debt is getting pricier. It is that the repricing is global. A bond market that sees deficits and inflation risk everywhere can impose discipline everywhere too — sometimes with more speed than any elected government would choose.

### A USPS whistle-blower raises alarms over mail voting before the midterms  
*Source: [NYT U.S.][usps-voting]*

A whistle-blower report is warning of “grave concerns” inside the U.S. Postal Service over a new system being rushed into use before the midterm elections. The allegation, as described, is that USPS is secretly proceeding on President Trump’s mail-voting plan — and that the rollout could put millions of mail ballots at risk.

The excerpt does not explain the mechanics of the system. That absence is important to keep in view: the accusation is stark, but the operational details are not included here. Still, the shape of the concern is clear enough. Election administration depends heavily on logistics, and mail voting in particular depends on ordinary competence: predictable handling, clear procedures and trust that the pipeline will work.

A postal change can look bureaucratic until it intersects with ballots. Then it becomes constitutional infrastructure.

### The Supreme Court is writing history — then sealing the drafts  
*Sources: [NYT U.S.][scotus-records], [NYT Video][scotus-video]*

The Supreme Court’s public work is monumental; its internal record may remain hidden for decades. After Justice Antonin Scalia died, the justices privately negotiated how long to lock away their papers, according to the reporting. A companion video frames the stakes directly: these materials could shape how future generations understand major decisions by the nation’s highest court.

The court’s opinions tell the official story. Internal papers tell a different kind of history — how arguments evolved, how coalitions formed, what concerns were raised and abandoned. That does not mean every draft should be public in real time. But sealing the record for decades changes the balance between institutional privacy and democratic memory.

The Roberts court is deciding major questions in American life. If the working record stays locked away, historians and the public will be left reading the era through a keyhole — long after the consequences have already arrived.

## The Machine Room

### Open-source AI projects are starting to say: please stop sending PRs  
*Source: [Latent Space][prs-not-welcome]*

Some prominent AI-adjacent open-source projects are rethinking one of open source’s old rituals: the outside pull request. Vercel’s AI SDK, Astro, Flue and tldraw are described as moving away from drive-by community contributions and toward “software factories,” where teams of agents apply fixes and features.

The framing — “PRs NOT Welcome” — is deliberately provocative. But underneath it is a practical maintainer problem. A successful project can attract so many one-off contributions that reviewing, triaging and integrating them becomes its own full-time burden. In that world, orchestrated automation may start to look cleaner than a crowded inbox.

If this shift sticks, it changes the social meaning of open source. External pull requests have long been treated as proof of vitality: people care enough to help. Agent-driven pipelines move the center of gravity back toward maintainers, who can direct automated contributors rather than negotiate with thousands of humans. That may make projects more efficient. It may also make them less communal.

## Back Page

### John Boorman revisits why *Exorcist II* became a legendary flop  
*Source: [NYT Movies][exorcist-ii]*

John Boorman is looking back at one of cinema’s better-known misfires: *Exorcist II*. A new documentary aims to reassess the 1977 failure, and Boorman apparently does not dodge the obvious issue. One explanation offered in the excerpt is almost brutally simple: it was not really scary.

For a sequel to *The Exorcist*, that is not a small flaw. It is close to a category error.

But that is also why flops remain interesting. Successful films often get flattened into inevitability; disasters keep their seams visible. A reassessment can show how ambition, genre expectations and audience memory collide. Sometimes a movie fails because it is careless. Sometimes because it is trying to be something its title will not allow.

### A rare good-news number: 325 kākāpō  
*Source: [Simon Willison][kakapo]*

Here is the day’s palate cleanser, and a real one: the kākāpō population has reached 325. Andrew Digby notes that chicks from this year’s record breeding season are now juveniles and have been added to the total.

The comparison is the story. In 1995, only 51 kākāpō remained. Now there are 325.

Conservation news is often written as a chronicle of managed decline: fewer habitats, fewer animals, fewer chances. This is a reminder that sustained recovery work can bend the curve the other way. Not quickly, not cheaply, not by accident — but actually.

Digby’s conclusion is worth letting stand: recovery of critically endangered species is possible with sustained effort.
