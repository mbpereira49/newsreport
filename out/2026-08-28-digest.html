Prediction markets get pulled back toward the gambling table

A new appeals court ruling deepens a legal split over whether event contracts are finance, betting, or both.

An appeals court said prediction markets should be regulated as gambling, contradicting an earlier appeals court decision. Federal regulators, according to the Times excerpt, see the split as Supreme Court material. The uncertainty is the story: prediction-market companies have tried to present themselves as information engines and financial exchanges, while courts are now offering competing answers about whether wagers on real-world outcomes belong under gambling law.

Why it matters: If the Supreme Court takes the issue, it could define the boundaries for a fast-growing market that sits awkwardly between finance, politics, sports-style betting, and public forecasting.

China pitches open AI as the safer AI

A Chinese start-up is using recent U.S. security scares to argue against Silicon Valley’s closed-model approach.

Why it matters: The AI race is no longer just about model quality. Safety narratives are becoming geopolitical branding, and “open” versus “closed” is now a strategic argument as much as an engineering choice.

Security disclosure now has a minutes-long fuse

Simon Willison highlights a troubling report: even a rumor of a bug can trigger automated exploit hunting almost immediately.

Simon Willison points to a post by Anil Madhavapeddy, a Cambridge computer science professor and OCaml compiler maintainer, describing a sharply compressed vulnerability timeline. Where security issues once allowed days for coordination and perhaps a week or two for release, Madhavapeddy reports probes arriving within about ten minutes of patches being shared for discussion. The excerpt suggests automated watchers are scanning public repositories, while modern coding agents have become effective enough at finding flaws that even partial public signals can be dangerous.

Why it matters: Open-source security depends on collaboration, but collaboration leaves traces. If attackers can turn patch discussions into exploit attempts within minutes, maintainers may need new norms for private coordination, embargoes, and automated defense.

Sources: Simon Willison

A Spanish museum heist fits Europe’s faster, rougher art-theft pattern

Thieves stole Bronze Age treasures from the Museum of Villena in a robbery experts compare to recent violent smash-and-grab tactics.

Thieves stole Bronze Age treasures from Spain’s Museum of Villena in what art-theft experts describe as part of a broader European pattern: robberies that are quick, brazen, and increasingly violent. The Times excerpt says the heist may have been inspired by last year’s Louvre break-in, though that remains framed as expert interpretation rather than established fact. Why it matters: Museums are built for public access, not fortress conditions. If high-profile heists become templates, institutions holding portable antiquities may face new pressure to harden security without turning cultural spaces into bunkers.
