India’s China problem is not just geopolitical; it is embedded in supply chains. The Times reports that trade between India and China has nearly doubled in five years, leaving New Delhi with a widening deficit and an uncomfortable choice about how to build its industrial future. The tension is familiar but acute: reducing dependence on Chinese imports sounds strategically tidy, while actually manufacturing at scale often means buying more Chinese inputs, machinery, or components. The excerpt does not spell out which sectors are driving the increase, but the core contradiction is clear: industrial self-strengthening can, at least in the near term, deepen the dependency it is meant to escape.

Why it matters: India’s manufacturing ambitions are central to global supply-chain diversification. If building alternatives to China requires more Chinese imports first, decoupling becomes less a switch than a long, politically awkward transition.

Migration season brings a simple civic ask: turn off the lights

Experts say artificial light draws birds into buildings, contributing to more than a billion collision deaths each year.

As millions of birds migrate, residents are being asked to dim or turn off lights that can pull birds off course and into danger. The Times cites experts who say birds are drawn to lights and that more than a billion die each year after crashing into buildings. The appeal is striking because the intervention is so low-tech: no grand restoration plan, no exotic equipment—just darker windows and skylines at the right moment. The story is also a reminder that cities are habitats whether they intend to be or not, and that design choices made for humans can become lethal signals for other species.

Why it matters: Bird migration is a continental-scale event running through everyday neighborhoods. Light reduction is one of the rare conservation measures that is immediate, cheap, and available to ordinary buildings.

Sources: NYT U.S.
