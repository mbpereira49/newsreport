Rural Alaska’s energy math gets harsher

In places like Manley Hot Springs, electricity still runs through diesel generators—and the bill is rising.

The squeeze in rural Alaska is brutally simple: small towns and hamlets rely on diesel generators for electricity, and diesel prices are soaring. The Times frames Manley Hot Springs as a community under strain from an energy crisis worsened by war, with “no end in sight” to the price pressure. That means this is not just an inflation story; it is an infrastructure story, too. When the basic system for keeping lights on depends on fuel that must be bought at volatile global prices, every geopolitical shock lands locally.

Why it matters: Energy transition debates often sound abstract. In diesel-dependent communities, they show up as immediate questions of survival, affordability and whether a place can remain viable.

A claimed crack in hidden AI reasoning

A paper highlighted by Simon Willison says encrypted chain-of-thought blocks can be replayed and exposed.

Simon Willison points to a paper with a memorable vanity domain, stolen-thoughts.com, and a striking claim: Anthropic, OpenAI and Google return encrypted chain-of-thought blocks to clients that can be replayed across sessions, users and models. The excerpt says the researchers take a trace from a frontier model, replay it into a weaker sibling, jailbreak that weaker model and recover the stronger model’s hidden reasoning in plaintext. That is a big claim, and the excerpt frames it as a “neat paper,” not a fully litigated security consensus. Still, the alleged failure mode is serious: hidden reasoning may be less hidden if implementation details allow replay attacks.

Why it matters: As model providers hide chain-of-thought for safety and product reasons, the security of those hidden traces becomes part of the API surface—not just an internal design choice.

Sources: Simon Willison

Silicon Valley loses a different kind of maker

The Times profiles Andy Mariani, an 80-year-old grower of “exotic and ethereally delicious” stone fruit in Silicon Valley. The hook is elegiac: a dirt farmer hanging up his shears in a region better known for code, capital and campuses. The excerpt says he is giving up the land, but does not specify what will replace it. Even without that detail, the contrast is sharp. Why it matters: Stories like this mark how economic geography changes culture. When agricultural land disappears from high-value regions, what vanishes is not only acreage but knowledge, flavor and memory.
