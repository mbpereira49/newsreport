Pentagon casualty reporting gets harder to read

A change in how Iran war casualties are posted separates figures across two web pages, potentially obscuring the full toll.

The Pentagon has changed the way it reports casualty numbers from the Iran war, splitting the figures into two separate web pages. The practical effect, as framed by the report, is not a revision of the toll itself but a presentation change that may make the total harder to tally. That distinction matters: transparency can erode through formatting as much as through omission.

Why it matters: In wartime, casualty accounting is one of the public’s basic tools for judging costs. Making the count more cumbersome weakens oversight even if the underlying numbers remain available.

05

The gray market reselling discounted LLM access

A relay economy is pooling API keys and routing users through proxies, with discounts allegedly powered by free-trial abuse, exposed bots and fraud.

Simon Willison highlights Matt Lenhard’s investigation into a relay market for reselling LLM tokens at a discount. The model, as described, is straightforward and ugly: resellers sell access to an LLM proxy, then source cheaper or illicit capacity by pooling API keys, abusing free trials, proxying through unprotected support bots, or using stolen credit cards and chargeback attacks. The activity appears to be mostly in China. The tooling complicates the picture: one-api and its fork new-api are described as open-source, legitimate API proxy products, even if some operators use them for abuse.

Why it matters: AI pricing, API security and fraud are now entangled. If discounted “LLM access” is subsidized by abuse, providers will keep tightening controls—and legitimate proxy and aggregation tools may get caught in the blast radius.

Sources: Simon Willison

The push to restart a shuttered nuclear reactor in the United States is proving harder than the pro-revival pitch can make it sound. According to the report, the country’s first such attempt has been held back by run-down equipment and a lack of planning. That is a useful corrective to the clean-energy shorthand around nuclear restarts: dormant infrastructure has a long memory, and neglect becomes a project cost.

Why it matters: As nuclear power gets renewed attention, reopening old plants may look faster than building new ones. This case suggests speed depends less on ambition than on maintenance, planning and the condition of the machinery left behind.
