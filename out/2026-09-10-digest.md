## The Big Pattern: AI’s Off-Balance-Sheet Costs

### The AI boom’s power bill is turning into a health bill  
*[NYT Business](https://nytimes.com/2026/09/10/climate/ai-data-centers-air-pollution-health.html)*

The data-center buildout is usually narrated in the clean language of capacity: chips, racks, megawatts, latency, cloud regions. A warning from former E.P.A. officials gives it a less abstract frame: air pollution from gas-fired power plants serving those facilities could produce at least **$20 billion a year in health costs by 2028**.

That number is doing more than adding drama. It challenges the industry’s preferred vocabulary around “AI infrastructure,” which tends to make the physical world disappear. The electricity has to come from somewhere. If it comes from gas plants, the consequences may show up not just in utility planning documents but in asthma rates, hospital visits, premature deaths and public-health budgets.

**Why it matters:** AI’s costs are increasingly landing outside tech balance sheets — on grids, local air quality and health systems. The fight over “clean” AI may become less about corporate climate pledges and more about who breathes what near the power supply.

### San Francisco reaches for emergency powers as AI pressure hits rents  
*[NYT U.S.](https://nytimes.com/2026/09/10/us/san-francisco-daniel-lurie-rent-emergency.html)*

San Francisco Mayor Daniel Lurie plans to declare a rent emergency as the city faces the nation’s highest rent increases amid an AI-driven housing frenzy. The familiar Bay Area loop is tightening again: a lucrative tech boom, a constrained housing market, rising rents, then political alarm once displacement becomes too visible to ignore.

Emergency declarations can be useful. They can also be theatrical. The real test is whether the city can slow evictions and soften rent pressure in a market where new demand arrives faster than policy can move. San Francisco has been through versions of this story before, but AI appears to be giving it a fresh velocity.

**Why it matters:** AI’s local footprint is not just office leases, model training and high salaries. In boom cities, it can translate almost immediately into rent pressure, eviction risk and a civic argument over who gets to remain.

### A zero-click WeChat worm demo shows how AI changes exploit work  
*[Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Sep/10/calif-research/)*

Simon Willison highlights a striking claim from Calif Research: a demo worm called **WeWorm** that spreads through WeChat calls across iOS and Android without the victim answering or touching the phone. The researchers say AI helped them find the bug and write a first remote-code-execution exploit in roughly two days, with the worm taking another week.

This is framed as a demo, not a reported outbreak in the wild. That distinction matters. Still, the claim points toward the uncomfortable part of AI-assisted security work: acceleration cuts both ways. The same tools that help defenders summarize logs and write detection rules may also compress the timeline for serious offensive research.

The researchers’ broader point is less “AI did it all” than “AI made the hard parts faster.” Humans still supplied target selection, engineering judgment and safety boundaries. But if a months-long exploit chain can become a days-or-weeks project for a smaller team, the security calendar changes.

**Why it matters:** AI is not merely helping people write cybersecurity reports. If the claim holds, it is shortening the path from vulnerability discovery to working exploit — and that puts pressure on patching, disclosure norms and platform security teams.

## Speech, Maps and the Machinery of Power

### Kimmel takes an interview off broadcast TV after an F.C.C. warning  
*[NYT Business](https://nytimes.com/2026/09/10/business/jimmy-kimmel-james-talarico-fcc.html)*

Jimmy Kimmel told viewers that ABC moved an interview with James Talarico to YouTube because the F.C.C. had “threatened” the network over “traditional editorial decisions.” The available account gives Kimmel’s framing, not the regulator’s response, so the precise nature of the alleged pressure remains unclear.

But the move itself is revealing. A late-night program treated a platform shift as the safer editorial route. Broadcast television still lives under a regulatory framework that digital platforms largely do not, and this episode turns that difference into a programming decision.

It is also a reminder that “just put it online” is not merely a distribution strategy. It can become a pressure valve when institutions decide the risk calculus is different on YouTube than on a broadcast network.

**Why it matters:** The story sits at the uneasy intersection of political speech, broadcast regulation and the migration of TV moments to less regulated digital spaces. The question is not only what was said, but where it was deemed safe to say it.

### The Supreme Court leaves Missouri’s GOP map blocked — again  
*[NYT U.S.](https://nytimes.com/2026/09/10/us/politics/supreme-court-missouri-congress.html)*

The U.S. Supreme Court again declined to revive Republican-favored congressional lines in Missouri, leaving in place the state Supreme Court’s ruling against the map. The Missouri court had said voters must first have a chance to approve the district lines in a statewide referendum.

That makes the dispute procedural, but not minor. Redistricting fights often look like technical battles over boundaries, timelines and standards of review. Underneath, they decide who has a realistic shot at power for years. Here, the added question is who gets the last word before a map takes effect: lawmakers, courts or voters.

The U.S. Supreme Court’s move does not end the larger fight. It does, however, show how state constitutional rules and referendum processes can complicate national partisan strategies.

**Why it matters:** Redistricting cases can shape congressional power long before voters cast ballots. This one also underscores the role of state courts as friction points in national political planning.

### Tariff compliance, now with sprinkles  
*[NYT Business](https://nytimes.com/2026/09/10/business/economy/canada-tariffs-sprinkles.html)*

The practical chaos of tariffs often hides inside classification codes, customs forms and procurement spreadsheets. Here it surfaces in a beautifully absurd question from businesses trying to decode President Trump’s new levies on Canadian goods: **“Are sprinkles on the list?”**

That one line captures the lived reality of trade policy. Tariffs may be announced as national strategy, but the burden quickly becomes granular. Companies have to determine whether their ingredients, inputs, packaging or finished products are covered. The uncertainty can be as expensive as the tariff itself, especially when supply chains were not built to be reclassified overnight.

The policy is broad; the compliance work is microscopic. Somewhere, someone is matching dessert decorations against a tariff schedule.

**Why it matters:** Tariffs do not just raise prices at the border. They create administrative drag, surprise costs and planning uncertainty deep inside ordinary commerce.

## After the Front Page

### Fish Cheeks still earns its place in New York’s crowded Thai scene  
*[Eater NY](https://ny.eater.com/dining-report/413700/fish-cheeks-nyc-thai-restaurant-review-nolita-seafood) · [Eater New York](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMimwFBVV95cUxNRHVZQjlJWW1MeWtVanNnNUZJMlpzUmo2anNkTUl4ZF9RMHdmYy1MOFoybWpxU2RBYlY2WTI5cV9KU2RpSUNjWnRmSlNTS3QzRE1XdTBJV3MwdWNsQ0Q3UERKZ05xY1NLZnl4bE5GeUhuSEVTX1BDX1lWcEU5R1NOZ0VpVXhUcC1GVnI4RjljcTd5T1pDVlotWTJPVQ?oc=5)*

New York has enough excellent Thai food that staying relevant for a decade is not automatic. Eater’s revisit of **Fish Cheeks**, the Nolita Thai seafood restaurant opened in 2016 by brothers and chefs Ohm and Chat Suansilphong with Jenn Saesue, argues that it still has the thing many restaurants lose: a clear point of view.

That point of view includes Thai seafood, a bright room and a menu that never leaned on pad Thai as a shortcut to legibility. In a city where restaurants age quickly — first hyped, then overexposed, then quietly replaced in the discourse — “still relevant” is meaningful praise.

The larger pleasure of the piece is its attention to restaurants as long-running arguments, not just openings. Fish Cheeks mattered because it knew what it wanted to be. Apparently, it still does.

**Why it matters:** Restaurant influence is not only about debuts and waitlists. It is also about maintaining a voice after the hype cycle has moved on.

### Los Angeles gives P-22 a bronze afterlife  
*[NYT U.S.](https://nytimes.com/2026/09/10/us/p-22-los-angeles-mountain-lion-sculpture.html)*

P-22, the mountain lion who became a Los Angeles civic icon, will be memorialized in bronze in Griffith Park. The location is the point: this is where he roamed before his death in 2022, turning an urban park into the stage for an improbable cross-species celebrity story.

The sculpture sounds less like a conventional monument than a marker of affection. P-22’s fame came from contradiction. He was wild, but deeply urban; solitary, but somehow communal; a predator who became a mascot for coexistence in a city built across habitat.

Los Angeles has many symbols of ambition, glamour and sprawl. This one is quieter: a reminder that wildness persists in the margins and corridors of the city, sometimes close enough to become beloved.

**Why it matters:** Public monuments usually reveal whom a city wants to remember. This one suggests Los Angeles also wants to remember the nonhuman life threaded through it.
