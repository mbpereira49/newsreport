## The AI Stack Gets More Serious

### Stateless MCP gives the agent-tooling spec a second wind

Simon Willison says the July 28 update to the Model Context Protocol may be the biggest shift since MCP’s debut — and enough to pull him back in. [Source: Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Jul/31/stateless-mcp/)

MCP began as Anthropic’s attempt to standardize how tools are exposed to LLM-powered agent frameworks. It had a strong 2025 moment, then was partly overshadowed by Anthropic’s Skills. Now, in Willison’s telling, the new stateless direction changes the feel of the thing: less brittle, more practical, worth building against again.

That matters because practitioner enthusiasm is often a better signal than protocol rhetoric. Willison says the update has already inspired **mcp-explorer** and **datasette-mcp**, which is the kind of concrete response specs dream of producing. It suggests MCP is not merely being revised, but becoming useful in a way that skeptical builders can feel.

**Why it matters:** Agent infrastructure is still unsettled. If MCP can win back hands-on developers, the ecosystem may be inching toward more durable interfaces between models, tools, and data.

### Open models keep crowding the frontier

Interconnects’ latest “open artifacts” roundup points to Laguna S2.1, Inkling, and Kimi K3 as signs that strong model training capacity is spreading. [Source: Interconnects](https://interconnects.ai/p/latest-open-artifacts-23-laguna-s21)

The thesis is blunt: **“Capacity to train strong models is proliferating.”** The post frames these models as useful on the Pareto frontier — the uncomfortable zone where trade-offs between capability, cost, access, and deployment become real strategic questions rather than benchmark theater.

The excerpt is spare, but the direction is notable. Open-model progress is no longer just about symbolic availability, imitation, or trailing the closed labs by a respectable distance. It is increasingly about credible artifacts that test how much of the leading edge can live outside proprietary walls.

**Why it matters:** If more groups can train genuinely strong models, advantage shifts. Owning raw capability is still valuable, but distribution, tooling, data, trust, and deployment become harder to fake — and harder to monopolize.

### AI spending now has its own ROI discipline: tokenomics

Companies are pouring money into AI, and a new measurement field is trying to answer the obvious question: what are they getting back? [Source: NYT Business](https://nytimes.com/2026/08/03/business/economy/ai-spending-tokenomics.html)

The New York Times reports that “tokenomics” has emerged as a way to measure returns on corporate AI spending. The label is doing a lot of work, but the shift underneath it is real. After the first wave of demos, pilots, and infrastructure bills, AI is entering the spreadsheet phase.

That means counting tokens purchased, workflows changed, time saved, tasks automated, money conserved — or not. The supplied excerpt does not say whether tokenomics is a rigorous discipline, a consultant-friendly buzzword, or something in between. But the pressure is unmistakable: the finance side of the house wants AI to justify itself.

The interesting tension is that AI value is often indirect. A tool may speed up routine work, improve search, help engineers debug, or make customer support less painful. Translating that into clean ROI is hard. But budgets eventually force the question.

**Why it matters:** The winners may not be the flashiest AI products. They may be the ones that can prove they are worth the bill.

## Culture at Platform Speed

### AI speeds up the corrido as breaking-news memorial

The New York Times reports that AI is helping people write corridos within hours, compressing a century-old tradition into the tempo of social media. [Source: NYT U.S.](https://nytimes.com/2026/08/03/us/ai-corridos-song-music-texas-ice-shooting.html)

The case in the headline is stark: **“ICE Killed Him.”** The story appears to center on a Texas ICE shooting and the rapid creation of a folk ballad in response. What is striking is not simply that AI can help produce lyrics. We already know it can do that. The deeper point is that a communal form of remembrance, protest, and storytelling can now move almost immediately after tragedy becomes public.

That speed changes the role of the song. A corrido has long served as memory, argument, and witness. With AI in the loop, it can become near-instant commentary — a memorial arriving before institutions have investigated, explained, or settled the narrative.

There is power in that, and risk. Speed can preserve emotion while it is still raw. It can also harden an interpretation before facts have stabilized. Either way, generative AI is not staying inside offices, code editors, or productivity suites. It is entering vernacular culture.

**Why it matters:** AI’s cultural impact may be most visible not in polished entertainment, but in the everyday forms people use to grieve, accuse, remember, and rally.

### Misty Copeland curates ballet after the spotlight

Misty Copeland, the first Black female principal dancer at American Ballet Theater, is now curating a two-week festival at the Joyce Theater. [Source: NYT Arts](https://nytimes.com/2026/08/03/arts/dance/misty-copeland-ballet-festival-joyce-theater.html)

The Times frames the piece around both the festival and Copeland’s ballet philosophy, which suggests a post-performance role more substantial than celebrity stewardship. The excerpt does not give many program details, but the premise is enough: a dancer whose career carried institutional and symbolic weight is now shaping what appears onstage.

Curation is quieter than performance, but it can be just as consequential. A festival decides who is visible, which lineages matter, which risks are worth taking, and what kind of future an art form imagines for itself. Copeland’s influence now moves through selection, framing, and taste.

**Why it matters:** Institutions do not change only through appointments or premieres. They also change through programming — through the repeated act of deciding what deserves the room.

### Spider-Man is still built for blockbuster gravity

Sony’s **“Spider-Man: Brand New Day”** was on pace for an $875 million global opening, according to the Times. [Source: NYT Movies](https://nytimes.com/2026/08/01/business/media/spider-man-box-office-superhero.html)

The number is the obvious story. But so is the count: this is the 11th big-budget Spider-Man movie since 2002. Every few months, Hollywood seems to rediscover the possibility of superhero fatigue. Then a property like Spider-Man reminds the industry why studios remain addicted to known masks, logos, and multigenerational IP.

The careful wording here is “heads for,” not final tally. The supplied excerpt says the film was tracking toward one of the biggest domestic first weekends ever and an $875 million global opening. Still, the signal is clear enough: some franchises still bend the market around themselves.

**Why it matters:** The franchise economy may be creatively strained, but it is not economically dead. Familiar characters can still generate blockbuster gravity when the machinery works.

## Power, Money, and Fragile Systems

### Trump’s Justice Department pressure is creating legal blowback

The New York Times frames President Trump’s involvement in the Justice Department as self-defeating: one case imperiled, another exposed to sanctions, and a nomination complicated. [Source: NYT U.S.](https://nytimes.com/2026/08/03/us/trump-doj-comey-reflecting-pool-blanche.html)

The excerpt describes an unusually compressed chain of consequences in a single week. Trump’s interference reportedly imperiled one criminal case, set the stage for sanctions in a second, and hindered the confirmation chances of his pick for attorney general.

The details of the cases are not included here, but the pattern is familiar enough: political pressure meant to control legal outcomes can instead contaminate them. Courts operate on process. Prosecutors need independence not as a decorative norm, but because tainted process can become a legal vulnerability.

The editorial point is simple: meddling does not merely look bad. It can make cases harder to win, easier to challenge, and more damaging to the people applying the pressure.

**Why it matters:** Justice Department independence is not abstract when interference affects prosecutions, sanctions risk, and confirmation politics. In law, process often becomes outcome.

### The U.S. steps in as the yen’s slide becomes a wider market risk

The U.S. Treasury has joined efforts in Tokyo to stem the yen’s slide against the dollar, according to the Times. [Source: NYT Business](https://nytimes.com/2026/08/03/business/us-japan-yen-intervention.html)

Currency interventions are rarely just about a number on a screen. The excerpt points to “broader risks” from turmoil in Japanese markets, and the U.S. role is the key signal. If Washington is helping support the yen, officials likely see potential spillovers beyond Japan — or at least enough disorder to justify coordination.

The yen’s weakness matters because Japan is deeply embedded in global finance. A sharp or disorderly move can affect bonds, trade, corporate balance sheets, and investor positioning. Currency markets have a way of turning national problems into international ones when leverage and confidence start moving together.

**Why it matters:** Japan is not a side pocket of the global economy. A disorderly yen can ripple outward quickly, especially when investors are already positioned for one version of the future and markets begin pricing another.

## Closing Thought

Today’s stories are mostly about second-order effects. AI needs ROI metrics. AI-generated songs carry grief faster than institutions can respond. A legal intervention creates legal weakness. A currency slide becomes a global concern. A dancer’s influence continues through curation rather than performance.

The first wave is the event. The second wave is what the system does with it.
