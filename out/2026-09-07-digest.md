## Memory, Inherited

### The post-9/11 generation now has its own view of 9/11  
*Young adults too young to remember the attacks still carry opinions about their legacy, shaped by absence, security lines and inherited memory.*  
Source: [NYT New York Region](https://nytimes.com/2026/09/07/nyregion/born-after-9-11-young-adults.html)

They never saw the pre-9/11 skyline. They do not remember airports before the security regime. But young adults born after the attacks have strong feelings about how the country should regard 9/11’s legacy.

That makes the anniversary less a memory test than a transfer of meaning. What happens to a defining national trauma when the people coming of age know it through rituals, family stories, school lessons, wars, surveillance architecture and airport routines — but not through the day itself?

The question is not whether the event still matters. It plainly does. The question is who gets to define its public meaning now: those who remember the shock in real time, or those who inherited the world built in response.

**Why it matters:** Generational distance changes public memory. As 9/11 moves from lived event to inherited history, debates over security, grief, war and national identity will increasingly be led by people formed by its aftermath, not its day.
