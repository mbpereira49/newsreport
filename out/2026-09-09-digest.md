## The Big Fights

### Trump’s mail-voting plan heads straight into a constitutional fight

The Trump mail-voting plan, as described by the Times, would add new requirements around who is eligible to receive mail ballots. That is the operational piece. The political and legal piece is already moving: two dozen states have challenged the plan as unconstitutional.

The mechanics are still thin from the available excerpt, but the collision is not. A federal push to reshape mail-ballot access is being met by states arguing that the plan overreaches into territory they control.

Mail voting has become much more than an administrative question. It is now a proxy battlefield over access, fraud claims, federal authority and state power. The immediate question is who gets a ballot. The larger one is who gets to write the rules before voting is already underway.

### Salvadorans keep TPS, but only “for now”

Temporary Protected Status for Salvadorans will continue for now, according to Trump administration officials, despite having been set to expire Wednesday.

That phrase—*for now*—is doing a lot of work. The administration did not give a final policy direction in the excerpt, saying only that it would announce the program’s future at the “appropriate time.” For recipients, that means immediate expiration has been avoided, but long-term uncertainty remains intact.

TPS is designed as temporary relief, but for many immigrants it becomes the framework around which whole lives are built: jobs, families, mortgages, schools, neighborhoods. A non-decision can be stabilizing in the short term while still keeping people suspended between reassurance and disruption.

**Source:** [NYT U.S. — “Salvadorans to Keep Temporary Protected Status for Now, Trump Officials Say”](https://nytimes.com/2026/09/09/us/tps-el-salvador-immigrants-trump.html)

## Power, Money, Infrastructure

### The bond market shrugs off Treasury’s $6 billion move

Treasury’s $6 billion plan to buy back government bonds did not get the response officials presumably wanted. Instead, the 10-year yield rose to its highest level in three years—a move the Times says suggested investors were underwhelmed by the details.

Buybacks can be used to improve market functioning or ease borrowing pressure. But here, bond investors appear to be saying that a relatively modest repurchase plan is not enough to change the underlying story.

That matters well beyond the bond desks. Higher Treasury yields ripple into mortgage rates, corporate borrowing costs and the government’s own interest bill. When yields rise after an intervention meant to lower borrowing costs, it is a clean vote of skepticism. The market’s message: show us something bigger, clearer or more convincing.

**Source:** [NYT Business — “Bond Market Rebuffs Treasury’s $6 Billion Plan to Reduce Borrowing Costs”](https://nytimes.com/2026/09/09/business/economy/treasury-repurchase-bessent.html)

### California reopens the nuclear question

California, long identified with a move away from nuclear power, is reconsidering the boundary of that stance.

The state is weighing an extension for Diablo Canyon, its last nuclear plant, while also considering whether to allow construction of new reactors. The pressure point is clean energy: keeping the grid reliable while reducing emissions.

The excerpt does not say how far the proposals have advanced, but the change in what is politically thinkable is the story. Nuclear power has long sat awkwardly inside environmental politics—carbon-light, but freighted with concerns about safety, waste and cost. California’s choices tend to become national case studies. If a climate-forward state decides nuclear power is useful, or even necessary, for decarbonization, it could soften one of the environmental movement’s longest-running taboos.

**Source:** [NYT Business — “Why California Is Reconsidering Its Decision to Shun Nuclear Power”](https://nytimes.com/2026/09/09/business/energy-environment/california-diablo-canyon-nuclear-energy.html)

## AI, Knowledge, and the Long Game

### AI’s impact may be both imminent and century-long

The Interconnects piece frames AI not as a single adoption wave, but as a compounding revolution: early enough that many people may not yet feel it directly, powerful enough that its trajectory could stretch across generations.

That framing is useful because it resists two easy errors. One is declaring that everything has already changed because a new demo is dazzling. The other is assuming that uneven adoption means the technology is overblown. Both can be true in pieces: AI may be overhyped in the short run and underestimated in the long run.

The management problem for the AI industry is expectations. How do you communicate a technology’s likely depth without pretending the future arrives everywhere at once? How do companies, schools and governments adapt when the impact is real but unevenly distributed?

The timing question may be the real one. If people tune out because the pitch sounds inflated, institutions move too slowly. If institutions panic because the pitch sounds apocalyptic, they may adopt badly. Either way, the revolution compounds.

**Source:** [Interconnects — “When will average people feel AI’s impact?”](https://interconnects.ai/p/when-will-average-people-feel-ais)

### Terence Tao worries AI could make open problems scarce

A Terence Tao quote highlighted by Simon Willison captures a sharper anxiety inside AI’s effect on research culture: open science depends on researchers sharing unfinished ideas, but AI may change the incentives.

Tao describes “good, fruitful open problems” as something being mined in a “non-renewable fashion.” More strikingly, he warns that even a rumor of someone working on a problem can trigger AI-powered efforts to “flatten it” before the original project matures.

That is not just a math-world etiquette dispute. The health of research depends partly on a social bargain: people share leads, conjectures and promising directions because openness helps the field advance. But if AI systems make it easier to swarm partially formed ideas, researchers may become more secretive—not because openness stopped being virtuous, but because it became strategically dangerous.

The nightmare version is a scientific commons that still praises collaboration while quietly training everyone to hoard.

**Source:** [Simon Willison — “Quoting Terence Tao”](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Sep/9/terence-tao/)

## Records and Reckonings

### A huge 9/11 air-quality paper trail is now public

More than 170,000 pages of 9/11 air-quality documents were released Tuesday, including reports, contamination records and correspondence among city officials in the months after the attacks.

The available excerpt does not characterize what the documents reveal, so the immediate significance is archival rather than conclusive. But that is not minor. Public-health accountability often moves through paper trails: what was measured, what was known, who was told, and how risk was communicated.

For survivors, responders and residents, the question has long been not only what the air contained, but what authorities understood at the time. Documents like these can reshape accountability years later—not through a single dramatic revelation, necessarily, but by making the record harder to summarize away.

**Source:** [NYT New York Region — “What to Know About the 9/11 Air Quality Documents”](https://nytimes.com/2026/09/08/nyregion/what-to-know-sept-11-air-quality-documents.html)

## Culture as Discomfort Machine

### Elizabeth Holmes gets the Nathan Fielder treatment

The surprise is not just that Elizabeth Holmes sat for another major screen treatment. It is who is asking the questions.

“You Can See Everything” pairs the convicted Theranos executive with Nathan Fielder and filmmaker Lance Oppenheim, according to the New York Times. Deadline and Variety frame the film as a review item, while the Times emphasizes its disruptive effect at Telluride. From the available sourcing, we know more about the movie’s arrival and aura than about a settled critical consensus.

Holmes has already been turned into a symbol, a cautionary tale and a content franchise. Fielder’s involvement suggests this version may be less straightforward exposé than discomfort machine—an examination not only of Holmes, but of the audience’s appetite for seeing her again.

That may be the interesting question now. What do we want from another Holmes story: confession, punishment, insight, spectacle? Fielder’s best work tends to make viewers aware of their own expectations. This pairing sounds engineered to do exactly that.
