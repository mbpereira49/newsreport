Treasury is getting more hands-on in the bond market

Scott Bessent is recasting Treasury’s role in the market that sets the price of money everywhere.

The New York Times frames Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent’s latest moves as a reinvention of the government’s role in the world’s most important bond market. The stated aim: lower interest rates. The loaded phrase is “interventionist tactics,” which suggests a shift from merely issuing debt and communicating policy toward a more active effort to shape market outcomes. With only the excerpt available, the mechanics are not spelled out, but the direction is clear enough: Treasury is no longer being described as a passive participant in rates.

Why it matters: The Treasury market is the benchmark for mortgages, corporate borrowing, government financing and global risk pricing. If Washington is more openly trying to manage rates, investors will care not just about Fed policy, but about Treasury strategy as a market-moving force in its own right.

Walmart’s sales slowdown says the cautious consumer is real

Retailers keep finding the same paradox: household balance sheets may look fine, but shoppers are acting nervous.

Walmart posted its slowest sales growth in years, according to the Times, joining other large U.S. retailers in warning that Americans are spending more carefully. The interesting tension is that the consumer does not appear broken on paper: the excerpt notes that household balance sheets “seemed healthy.” But retail executives are reading behavior, not spreadsheets, and the behavior is caution. Walmart is usually where households stretch dollars, so a slowdown there is harder to dismiss as a luxury-sector wobble.

Why it matters: Consumer spending has been the economy’s shock absorber. If even big-box retail is seeing slower growth, the question shifts from whether Americans can spend to whether they feel confident enough to keep doing it.

Simon Willison kicks the tires on smolvm for unsafe code—and immediately hits a tooling wall

The appeal is obvious: run untrusted Python and JavaScript with hard limits. The first lesson is that the evaluation environment matters.

Simon Willison set Claude Fable 5, running in Claude Code for web, a practical research task: test smolmachines/smolvm as a fast, secure sandbox for untrusted Python and JavaScript. The goal was concrete—limit RAM and CPU time, protect against infinite loops, block network access, and allow filesystem access only to designated files, useful for user-provided data transformations. The test quickly ran into a problem: Claude Code for web could not run smol machines. That is not a verdict on smolvm; it is a reminder that sandbox research is often blocked by the sandbox you are already inside.

Why it matters: As agentic systems execute more user-supplied code, “safe enough” sandboxing becomes infrastructure, not a side quest. But evaluations need to prove not only the security model, but also whether the tools can be exercised in real deployment environments.

Sources: Simon Willison
