Iran sanctions run into the China problem

Trump wants to squeeze Tehran without blowing up a fragile truce with Beijing.

The Trump administration is trying to cut off Iran’s economic lifelines, but the pressure campaign is quickly becoming a China story. Coding with agents is becoming less about line-by-line review

Simon Willison argues the real skill is directing AI tools well—and verifying outcomes confidently.

Simon Willison’s latest note cuts against a common reflex in AI-assisted programming: reading every line the machine wrote. His argument is that productive use of coding agents depends on two linked skills: giving confident instructions and verifying that the resulting changes were made correctly. Sometimes that still means close code review. But, he writes, “eyeballing every line of code” has never been the most effective way to validate software changes. The implication is subtle but important: agentic coding shifts the developer’s job toward specification, test design, and validation strategy—not passive acceptance, and not necessarily exhaustive inspection.

Why it matters: As coding agents get more capable, the bottleneck moves from generation to verification. Teams that adapt their review practices may get more from these tools than teams that merely bolt AI onto old workflows.

Sources: Simon Willison

That language pushes the issue out of sports-page tragedy and into workplace-safety territory. Football’s harms are often treated as inherent to the game, but the OSHA framing asks whether “inherent” has become a loophole.

Why it matters: If professional football is treated more like a workplace than an entertainment product, the legal and regulatory stakes change. The question is not whether the sport is dangerous; it is who has responsibility to reduce that danger.

Nancy Smith’s case has moved from wrongful-conviction story to something even more unsettling: the fragility of exoneration. Now prosecutors are trying to reverse an order saying she was innocent, according to The New York Times. The competing frames are stark: for Smith, a declaration of innocence should mean an end; for prosecutors, the legal fight apparently remains open.

Why it matters: Exoneration is often presented as the justice system correcting itself. This case shows how provisional that correction can be when prosecutors continue to contest not just procedure, but innocence itself.

New York’s 911 outage was also a transparency failure

Officials did not tell the public after a partial breakdown of the emergency system last week.

New York City’s 911 system suffered a partial outage last week, and officials kept quiet about it, according to The New York Times. The outage itself is serious enough: emergency systems are supposed to be boringly reliable. But the nondisclosure is the sharper civic problem. When the public is not informed about a breakdown in emergency response, residents lose the chance to adjust behavior, seek alternatives, or simply understand the risk they were living through.

Why it matters: Public trust in emergency infrastructure depends on candor after failures. Silence may avoid panic in the short term, but it also makes every future assurance harder to believe.

It is the sort of city detail that most people step over until someone decodes it, after which the pavement starts looking like a working map.

Why it matters: Cities are full of visible systems we stop seeing. These markings are a reminder that urban life depends on quiet coordination between buried infrastructure and the people trying not to break it.
