Paul Weiss, once described as a leader of the legal resistance against President Trump, is now being cast as the firm that modeled Big Law’s retreat. The Times investigation’s central claim, as excerpted, is blunt: the firm “caved to White House pressure.”

The mechanics of that pressure are not detailed in the supplied excerpt. But the framing matters because this is not just a story about one prestigious firm changing its posture. It is about the legal industry watching a powerful peer bend — and learning from it.

Elite law firms sell independence as part of their identity. They are not merely vendors of technical expertise; they are supposed to be institutions capable of standing between clients and state power. If political pressure can move even the most gilded players, the consequences run beyond reputation.

If Big Law treats pressure as a business risk to be managed rather than a principle to be defended, the whole legal ecosystem shifts.

## The New Infrastructure

### The AI open-letter wars are really about who gets to define “safety”  
*Simon Willison reads a Microsoft-shepherded letter on open-weight AI as an attempt to head off U.S. restrictions on open models.*  
Source: [Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Aug/2/open-letters/)

Simon Willison’s roundup of recent AI open letters spotlights one especially revealing document: “Open Weights and American AI Leadership,” dated July 24 and shepherded by Microsoft. It was signed by 235 AI-adjacent companies, including NVIDIA, Amazon, Y Combinator, The Linux Foundation and, later, OpenAI.

Willison’s read is that the letter is “clearly” designed to counter U.S. government instincts to ban or limit open-weight models on safety grounds. That is the policy fight in miniature: are open models a democratic and competitive necessity, or a proliferation risk?

The excerpt also notes the counterargument to easy regulatory logic: closed models are not automatically safe. They can be breached or misused too. So the debate is not cleanly “safety versus risk.” It is a fight over which risks count, which business models are protected, and who gets to set the frame.

**Why it matters:** Open-weight models sit at the center of AI’s next policy phase. Restrictions could reshape research, startups, national competitiveness, and misuse prevention. The loudest voices in the room are not neutral observers.

### Baby monitors are growing into childhood data businesses  
*Nanit and similar start-ups already track infants through the night. The next ambition is tracking children for much longer.*  
Source: [NYT Business](https://nytimes.com/2026/08/02/business/smart-baby-monitors-nanit-owlet.html)

Smart baby-monitor companies such as Nanit and Owlet are being described less as gadget makers than as data companies with a longer view of childhood. The Times excerpt says these start-ups want to track children through the night — and soon, for much longer than that.

The pitch is easy to understand: reassurance, pattern recognition, a little more sleep for anxious parents. The discomfort is embedded in the same promise. Once monitoring becomes normal in infancy, the business incentive is to extend the relationship, gather more signals, and turn parental worry into a recurring data stream.

This is the familiar bargain of consumer tech, only applied before the subject can meaningfully consent.

**Why it matters:** Parents are often offered surveillance as care. The question is whether the convenience of baby tracking quietly creates long-running dossiers on children before they have any say in the matter.

### The next space race is for spectrum, not just orbits  
*Radio spectrum is becoming valuable invisible real estate, with SpaceX and governments jostling for control.*  
Source: [NYT Business](https://nytimes.com/2026/08/01/business/dealbook/spacex-spectrum-battle.html)

The battle over space infrastructure is increasingly about something you cannot see: radio spectrum. The Times frames spectrum as “invisible real estate,” a scarce and valuable resource that companies like SpaceX are now pursuing alongside governments.

The excerpt does not spell out the specific regulatory fight. Still, the stakes are plain enough. Satellites, connectivity, national systems, and commercial networks all depend on access to frequencies. And scarcity does not become less political just because the asset is intangible.

The old space race was about rockets, flags, and orbital reach. The next one may be decided by allocation tables, interference disputes, and who gets priority in the invisible lanes above us.

**Why it matters:** Spectrum allocation decides who can communicate from space, at what scale, and under whose rules. As private satellite networks grow more powerful, governments are no longer the only actors shaping the architecture overhead.

### Alaska’s six-mile surf ride comes with a warning label  
*The Turnagain Arm tidal bore can carry surfers for an hour — and kill them. Now popularity may force authorities to decide how much risk is tolerable.*  
Source: [NYT U.S.](https://nytimes.com/2026/08/02/us/bore-tide-surfing-alaska.html)

South of Anchorage, the tidal bore that races up the Turnagain Arm offers a strange and beautiful prize: a wave that can carry a surfer for six miles, potentially for an hour. The same phenomenon can also kill.

The Times frames the story around a familiar outdoor-recreation dilemma. A dangerous local secret becomes more visible. More people show up. The risks become less private. Officials may then feel pressure to intervene.

The excerpt does not say what restrictions are being considered, if any. But the tension is clear: personal risk, viral adventure culture, and public responsibility do not coexist neatly. One person’s rare experience can become someone else’s rescue operation.

**Why it matters:** As extreme recreation gets more visible, remote hazards become governance problems. The hard question is where to draw the line between letting people chase extraordinary experiences and preventing predictable deaths.
